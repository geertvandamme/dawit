What is MIDI? The notes explained

A guide for beginners, like What is sound? in the wave editor, opened from the piano roll (a ? or What is MIDI? button): what MIDI is and isn't, from the cable to the bytes, using the clip in the editor as the example.

Mostly done: What is MIDI? in the piano roll's header opens the guide with ten topics (what MIDI is, where it came from, the cable and its speed, messages byte by byte, note numbers and pitch, velocity, pedals and wheels, ticks and clock, MIDI files, your clip), each with a picture that uses the clip in the piano roll: its notes as messages lit at the playhead, a C major chord as bits on the wire, a 128-note keyboard to click (it plays on the clip's track and shows the message in hexadecimal and binary), the clip's velocities, and its MIDI file byte by byte. Still open: the computer keyboard showing its messages, turning the velocity of a held note up and down, a monitor for a connected MIDI controller, and controller lanes in the piano roll to show.

  • What it is: MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) carries no sound, only instructions: which key went down, how hard, when it came up, what the pedal and the knobs did. The same notes can play a piano, a synth or drums; that's why a MIDI clip can change instrument and an audio clip can't (ties in with Convert MIDI from one part type to another and audio to MIDI).
  • The history: before MIDI, every maker had its own connections (control voltage and gate, Roland's DCB, Oberheim's bus), so a Moog couldn't play a Roland. Dave Smith (Sequential Circuits) and Ikutaro Kakehashi (Roland) proposed one standard; it was shown in January 1983 at NAMM with a Prophet-600 playing a Jupiter-6. Then: General MIDI (1991, the same 128 sounds and drum map on every device, why a MIDI file plays the right instruments), the Standard MIDI File (1988), MIDI over USB, Bluetooth and networks, MPE (2018, a channel per note for expressive controllers like the Seaboard and LinnStrument) and MIDI 2.0 (2020: 32-bit resolution, two-way property exchange).
  • The connectors and the wire: the 5-pin DIN socket (only three pins used), IN, OUT and THRU, daisy chains, why it's a current loop with an optocoupler (no ground loops, no hum), the 31,250 bits per second serial speed and what that means (a three-byte note takes about 1 ms, so a big chord on one cable is spread over several milliseconds); the 3.5 mm TRS jacks of small gear (type A and B); USB-MIDI today.
  • The messages, byte by byte: a status byte (the first bit is 1) and data bytes (the first bit is 0, so 0-127); Note On (0x90 + channel, note number, velocity), Note Off, running status, velocity 0 as a note off; the 16 channels; Control Change (sustain pedal 64, mod wheel 1, volume 7, pan 10), Program Change, Pitch Bend (14 bits, centred at 8192), Aftertouch (channel and polyphonic); clock, start and stop (24 pulses per quarter note) and MIDI Time Code; System Exclusive. Shown live: the notes of the clip as the bytes they'd be sent as, highlighted as the playhead passes them.
  • Note numbers and pitch: note 60 is middle C (C3 or C4 depending on the maker, and why), 69 is A 440 Hz, every number is a semitone, the formula f = 440 · 2^((n − 69)/12) (ties in with What is sound?'s frequency topic), and the 128 notes against a piano's 88 keys.
  • Velocity and expression: velocity is how fast the key went down, not how loud; what the instrument does with it (louder, brighter, a different sample); why programmed notes all at velocity 100 sound mechanical (ties in with Humanize MIDI with AI); controllers drawn as lanes.
  • Time: MIDI on a cable has no timeline, things happen when they arrive; a sequencer or a MIDI file adds one with ticks per quarter note (PPQ), tempo and time signature; quantize and swing as moving notes on the grid (ties in with Note lengths, beats and bars).
  • MIDI files: the Standard MIDI File's chunks (a header, then tracks of delta-time events), type 0 and type 1, what DawIt's export writes and what an import would read (ties in with MIDI file import).
  • Play with it: a keyboard on screen and the computer keyboard (ties in with Play notes with the computer keyboard) that show the message each key press sends, in hex, binary and plain words; turning velocity up and down on a held note; a MIDI monitor for a connected controller.

LUFS explained: what loudness is and how it's measured

A guide like the others (gain staging, saturation, the stereo image) about LUFS, the unit every streaming service, mastering tool and loudness meter uses, with a live meter on the song so each idea can be seen and heard. Today The mastering chain, step by step only has one bullet about loudness; this is the full explanation it would link to.

Plan: plans/lufs-loudness-meter.md

  • Topics:
    • Why peaks don't tell you loudness: two sounds with the same peak level can sound very different in loudness (a snare hit against a sustained pad or a squashed master); the ear follows the energy over time, not the highest sample. Shown with two waveforms at the same peak and their LUFS next to them.
    • What LUFS is: Loudness Units relative to Full Scale, from the ITU-R BS.1770 standard (also called LKFS); 1 LU is 1 dB, so -14 LUFS is 14 dB under a full-scale reference. Always negative for music; closer to 0 is louder.
    • K-weighting: before measuring, the sound goes through a filter that turns the very low end down and the presence range (above about 2 kHz) up, roughly how the ear hears. Why a bass-heavy mix measures quieter than its meter suggests, and a bright one louder. Drawn as the filter curve over the song's spectrum.
    • Momentary, short-term, integrated: over 400 ms (what you hear right now), over 3 seconds (a phrase), and over the whole song; integrated leaves out silence and very quiet parts (gating), so a quiet intro doesn't pull the number down. The three drawn over time while the song plays.
    • Loudness range (LRA): how much the loudness moves between the quiet and loud parts of the song, in LU: a few LU for a squashed pop master, 10 or more for a film score or a dynamic acoustic song.
    • True peak: the peaks between the samples that appear after conversion or MP3/AAC encoding; why masters stop at about -1 dBTP rather than 0 dBFS, and why a sample-peak meter can say 0 dB while the true peak is over.
    • Targets and normalisation: Spotify, YouTube, Tidal and Amazon play back at about -14 LUFS, Apple Music at about -16, broadcast at -23 (EBU R128) or -24 (US), podcasts about -16. A louder master is turned down to the target, so a master squashed to -7 LUFS just sounds flatter, not louder, next to a dynamic one. Shown with Hear it normalised: the song as the platform would play it.
    • Loud masters and the loudness war: what's lost when chasing loudness (punch, transients, the difference between verse and chorus), why club and EDM masters are still often -9 to -6 LUFS, and how to judge it with the crest factor (peak against loudness).
    • Loudness while mixing: compare effects, tracks and reference songs at the same LUFS so louder doesn't fool the ear; why the mix doesn't need a loudness target, only headroom; a quick check of a track's or bus's short-term LUFS against the mix.
  • The meter: a loudness meter on the master (later on any track or bus): momentary and short-term as bars, integrated, LRA and true peak as numbers, a line of the last minute or two, and a target picker (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, broadcast, club) that colours the numbers and shows how much the platform would turn the song up or down. Reset with a click; measures the loop range or the whole song from the start.
  • As a built-in metering effect, DawIt Meter: the same meter as an effect that passes the sound through untouched (no latency, no change to the level), so it can be put anywhere in a chain: after the last effect of a track to see its level, on a bus, or last on the master. It adds a window on the shared fxui frame and a small chip in the device bar, and is saved with the project like any effect. Today the level meters, peak dB and the arrows between chips in the device bar show only sample peaks; there is no LUFS, true peak or correlation code yet.
    • Loudness: momentary, short-term and integrated LUFS, loudness range (LRA) and the loudest short-term value, with a target line and a Reset button (it also resets when the transport restarts).
    • Levels: sample peak and true peak (dBTP) with a hold and a red over-mark above -1 dBTP, RMS, and the crest factor (peak minus RMS, in dB), with a stereo bar per channel and a dB scale.
    • Stereo: a phase correlation meter (+1 is mono, 0 is unrelated, -1 is cancelling in mono) and a goniometer, the stereo balance, and the mid and side levels. Shared with the picture in A stereo tool effect: width, and mono below a frequency so there is one implementation of each.
    • Over time: a history line of the last minute or two (momentary and short-term), and optionally a spectrum under it. Where it fits, the weighting of the numbers is named in the tooltips ("K-weighted, ITU-R BS.1770-4").
    • Explaining it: every number has a tooltip with what it is, what it is for and a typical value ("a streaming master is about -14 LUFS, -1 dBTP"), and a What is a loudness meter? guide, or a page in this guide, with a Your song topic reading the master's numbers against the chosen target.
    • Build: a measuring engine class in src/engine (for example LoudnessMeter.{h,cpp}, no UI, no allocation on the audio thread, results handed to the UI through atomics) used by the effect, the offline Measure loudness, Analyze the mix and the podcast and mastering steps, so all show the same numbers; the effect follows A built-in effect or instrument in docs/recipes.md. The tests check the numbers against the EBU Tech 3341 and 3342 test signals, a full-scale sine against its known -3.01 LUFS, a mono-in-both-channels signal against correlation +1 and an inverted one against -1, and that the audio passes through bit-identical and the settings save and load.
  • Measuring offline: Measure loudness renders the song (or the loop) and gives the integrated LUFS, LRA, true peak and the loudest part, without listening in real time; the same numbers Bounce to WAV could show after exporting.
  • Try it: buttons in the guide that turn the master up or down, add the built-in compressor or saturation, or squash it with a limiter, while the meter and the waveform follow, and Match loudness to hear the before and after at the same LUFS.
  • How: BS.1770-4 in plain DSP (the two K-weighting biquads, mean square per 400 ms block with 75% overlap, the absolute gate at -70 LUFS and relative gate at -10 LU, LRA from the short-term values per EBU Tech 3342, true peak by 4x oversampling), checked against the EBU test signals in the tests.
  • Ties in with: The mastering chain, step by step (the targets and the limiter), A master track in the arrangement and the mixer (the meter on the master strip), Gain staging in the plugin chain (short-term LUFS next to the peaks, equal-loudness A/B), Analyze the mix and advise on changes and Compare the levels of the buses (both measure in LUFS), What is sound? The kinds of reverb, explained and shown: room, hall, chamber, plate, spring

What is reverb? gives the kinds of reverb one page of text. Each deserves its own topic, with a moving picture of the real thing and of what it does to a clap, so you can tell a plate from a hall by eye and by ear and know which one to reach for. DawIt Reverb would then offer each kind as a type of its own, not only as starting points.

  • Sixteen guide topics, with moving pictures, clap responses and a first-100-ms reflection strip for each kind, side-by-side comparison and separate clap auditions.
  • Starting points name their type, including Echo chamber and Spring guitar; Your track suggests settings from the track's name and the reverb on it.

Still open

  • Convolution with recorded impulse responses and models of particular units.
  • Shimmer, reverse and modulated tails built into DawIt Reverb.
  • A decay-per-frequency picture per kind, a menu item that also loads typical values, and a picture in the device-bar chip.
  • What it does: a topic per kind in What is reverb?, each with what it is, where it came from, how it sounds, what it's used for, and its typical settings:
    • Room: a small space, with strong early reflections close together and a short tail (0.3-0.8 s): drums, guitar amps, making a dry recording sound recorded;
    • Hall: a concert hall, with early reflections from far walls after 20-40 ms and a long, smooth tail (a symphony hall is about 2 s); strings, choirs, piano ballads;
    • Chamber: a hard, empty room that a loudspeaker plays into and a microphone picks up, as in the basements of Abbey Road and Capitol: a room's sound with a denser, more even tail;
    • Plate: the EMT 140 (1957), a steel sheet about 1 by 2 metres shaken by a driver, with a damping pad that sets the decay. No walls, so no early reflections: the tail is dense from the first millisecond. High frequencies travel faster across a plate than low ones, which gives it its bright shimmer. The vocal and snare reverb of the 1960s to 80s;
    • Spring: metal springs with a driver at one end and a pickup at the other, small and cheap enough for organs (Hammond first) and guitar amps (Fender, from the early 1960s). The wave runs back and forth along the spring, so the tail comes in repeats, and the springs spread its frequencies out into the "boing" and "drip" of surf guitar and dub;
    • Digital: delay lines feeding back into each other, from the EMT 250 (1976) and the Lexicon 224 (1978) to DawIt Reverb; any room, or one that couldn't exist;
    • Convolution: the recorded impulse response of a real place or a real unit, applied to the sound;
    • the effects built on them: gated (a big reverb cut short), reverse (the tail before the note), shimmer (the tail shifted up an octave) and modulated tails.
  • The pictures: for each kind, the thing itself drawn and moving when the clap plays: ripples spreading over a plate and the pickups on it; a wave running along a spring and bouncing back; the hall and the room from above with the first reflections drawn as rays (like the room picture DawIt Reverb already has); a chamber with its speaker and microphone. Under it, what one clap becomes: the reflections and the tail over time, the echo density building up (sparse at first in a room, dense at once in a plate), and the decay per frequency (which part dies first). A page Side by side puts the kinds next to each other on the same clap, with a button to hear each in turn.
  • In DawIt Reverb: a Type switch (Room, Hall, Chamber, Plate, Spring) that changes how the sound is made, not only the knobs: no early reflections and a fast build-up for the plate; repeats and a chirping, dispersed tail for the spring (a chain of all-pass filters, as in spring models); far-apart reflections for a hall. The starting points name their type, and the explainer says which type a sound is usually given and why ("a plate on the vocal: bright, dense and short of early reflections, so the words stay in front").
  • Explaining it: the facts with numbers (decay times, pre-delays, the plate's size and year) checked against well-known sources; the "your track" look says which type suits the track it's on (vocal, snare, guitar) and how the reverb on it is set.

Stems for a mixing or mastering engineer: what to export and how to hand it over

A guide to sending your song to a mixing or mastering engineer as stems: what a stem is, which ones to export, and how to prepare them so the engineer can start at once. Today the mastering guide mentions stems in one line of its delivery topic, and the routing guide explains bouncing; nothing walks you through handing a project over.

  • What it does: a guide in the Theory Guides window, with a right-click menu entry on File > Bounce to WAV... and on the track menu. Its Your song topic lists the tracks and buses of the open song and what each would give.
  • What it covers:
    • Stems for mixing and for mastering are not the same: a mixer wants every track, or the main groups (drums, bass, music, vocals), dry or with the effects that make the sound; a mastering engineer wants a finished stereo mix, and sometimes 4 to 8 stems when the mix needs a fix.
    • Preparing the export: all stems start at bar 1 (the same start point, so they line up), same length, no master limiter or loudness processing, headroom of about -6 dBFS peak on the mix, 24-bit WAV at the project's sample rate, no dither, no normalising.
    • What to leave in or out: reverb and delay returns printed with their track or as their own stem, parallel buses, sidechain compression and tempo-synced effects, which are printed so they sound as you meant.
    • Checking before you send: add the stems back together and compare with your mix (they should null to silence or sound the same), listen for clicks at the edges, and name files clearly (01_Kick.wav, Song_120bpm_Am.wav).
    • What to send with them: a reference track, the tempo and key, a rough mix as a WAV, notes on what you like or worry about, and the MIDI or session notes when it helps.
    • Working with the engineer: revisions, how many are normal, and keeping your own copy of the project.
  • What exists: Bounce to WAV with the selected tracks, bounce in place, and the peak report. Per-track and per-bus stems export is still open (see the bounce idea), and the guide is most useful once it exists.
  • Explaining it: questions it must answer: What are stems? Should I send stems or a stereo mix? What do I send to a mastering engineer? Why do my stems not sound like my mix? They become search words in the Theory Guides window.

    • Lines and phrases: lines usually come in 2- or 4-bar phrases that match the melody's phrases, verses of 4 or 8 lines, a breath at the end of each line, and some room left between lines; a line that runs over its phrase sounds rushed.
    • Rhyme: where rhymes go (the ends of lines) and the usual schemes: AABB (couplets), ABAB (alternating), XAXA (only lines 2 and 4 rhyme, the most common in pop and folk), ABCB, plus perfect rhymes (night / light), slant or near rhymes (home / alone, common in modern songs) and internal rhymes within a line (rap, fast verses).
    • Stress and meter: every word has stressed syllables (to-GE-ther, HAP-py), and songs put them on the strong beats (1 and 3 in 4/4, the downbeat of each bar) and the weak syllables in between; a stress on a weak beat sounds wrong (to-ge-THER) unless it's done on purpose. Shown by marking the stressed syllables over the beat grid.
    • Mapping words to a melody: one syllable per note (syllabic, most pop verses) or one syllable over several notes (melisma, held vowels in a chorus or soul and gospel runs); long notes on open vowels (oh, ah, day, free) because they're easy to sing and hold, short notes for consonant-heavy words; the highest or longest note of a phrase on its most important word; pickups (words before the downbeat, and I...) to lead into a line; and the melody's rhythm following how the words would be spoken.
    • Prosody: the words and music saying the same thing: a rising melody on hopeful words, a falling one on sad ones, a pause before the key line, major or minor colouring the meaning, an unstable chord under an unresolved line.
    • The hook and the title: a short, singable phrase, repeated, placed at the start or end of the chorus where the melody peaks, the line people remember.
    • Writing tips: show, don't tell (concrete images over abstract feelings), one idea per song, the point of view (first person, second person to someone, third person storytelling), contrast between verse and chorus (detail versus summary, lower versus higher), and rewriting to fit the melody rather than forcing the melody to fit the words.
    • Common structures with their lyric roles: verse-chorus (pop, rock), verse-pre-chorus-chorus, AABA (32-bar standards, the title in the A sections), the 12-bar blues (AAB: a line, the line again, an answer line), and strophic (folk: the same music, new verses), each tied to Song sections and song structure.
    • Check the song's own lyrics: when the lyrics track has words, the guide can point at lines with too many syllables for their bars, a stressed syllable on a weak beat (from a syllable dictionary such as CMUdict for English), the rhyme scheme it finds, and the chorus lines that differ between choruses.
  • Ties in with: Song sections and song structure, Export to PDF (lyrics under the chords), Score view (lyrics under the notes), WAV generation (vocal and instrumental) (a model that sings needs the words and their timing), Notebooks (project notes would no longer need to hold the lyrics) and Generate lyrics with AI.

Change the key: transpose the whole song, chords and all

Move the whole song to another key in one step: the key, every chord on the chord track and every MIDI note go up or down together, so the song sounds the same, only higher or lower. Singers do this all the time to fit a song to their voice ("it's too high for me, take it down a tone"), and guitarists to get easier chord shapes. A second, more musical choice changes the scale too: the same song in the parallel minor or major (C major to C minor), the way a happy tune is made sad.

  • What it does:
    • Change key... from the key display in the transport bar and the chord track's menu: pick the new key, or a number of semitones up or down (-6 to +6), and see the change before it happens ("C major to D major: up 2 semitones. C G Am F becomes D A Bm G");
    • the key, the chord track, the MIDI notes on every track and the scale filter all move by the same interval. Drum tracks stay as they are (their notes are drums, not pitches), and so does any track the user unticks in the dialog;
    • the closest direction is picked by default (up 2 rather than down 10), with up or down as a choice, and a warning when notes would leave the MIDI range or a range that a part usually plays in ("the bass goes below E1");
    • changing the mode: C major to C minor (or A minor to A major) writes each chord and note into the new scale by its degree: the 3rd, 6th and 7th go down a semitone, so C G Am F becomes Cm Gm Ab Eb. Notes outside the scale are moved by the same rule, and the dialog lists what changes;
    • audio clips can't follow yet: they stay where they are, and the dialog says so ("the recording on Track 3 will be in the old key"). Shifting the pitch of audio needs pitch shifting, still to come.
  • Explaining it: a Changing the key topic in the chord guide:
    • why transposing keeps the song the same: every chord keeps its roman numeral (I V vi IV in any key), only the letters change, which the circle of fifths shows;
    • how singers pick a key: the highest and lowest note of the melody, and a typical range ("most untrained voices are comfortable over about an octave and a half");
    • capo or transpose: a capo moves the sound up without new shapes, transposing changes the shapes (ties in with the capo in Play along);
    • changing the mode as a sound, with the song's own first chords played in major and minor.
  • How to build it:
    • pure functions in src/engine next to MusicalKey and ChordEditing: transpose a key, a chord map, a note list by semitones; move a chord or a note to another mode by scale degree; the shortest interval between two keys. Tested on the A minor pop song, with chords outside the key, notes at the edges of the MIDI range and the drum tracks left alone;
    • one engine change under the lock, so the song never plays half transposed, and a sendChangeMessage(); with undo when Undo and redo is there;
    • the dialog in src/ui, with the before and after chords in their colours.
  • What stays open: audio clips (pitch shifting), a key change in the middle of a song (the last chorus a tone up), and transposing only a selection.

Practise singing: record yourself and get advice

Singing along to your own song and hearing back what went wrong is hard on your own: you can't hear yourself well while singing, and you don't know what to listen for. DawIt could record a take on a vocal track, measure it, and give advice that points at the singing techniques guide.

  • How it works: arm a vocal track, sing along with the Sing along window, and when you stop, How did I sing? analyses the take against the song's melody (a MIDI part or the guide vocal) and the lyrics track.
  • What it measures: pitch per note against the melody, in cents (sharp or flat, and whether you drift flat as a phrase ends, which often means running out of breath); how you start notes (sliding up from below, scooping); timing against the beat (early or late, and on which words); breaths: where you breathed, how long each phrase was, and whether the long phrases had a place to breathe (from the lyrics' rests, as Singing along already finds them); level and consistency (words that drop away), vibrato (rate and depth), and the microphone: clipping, plosives and loud s sounds.
  • The advice: a short list, worst first, each with the place in the song and a link to the guide's page: "the chorus' last line goes 30 cents flat on 'night': you breathed 6 bars before; take a quick breath after 'go' (Breathing)"; "you start most high notes from below (Singing in tune)". Clicking a point plays that moment of the take and the melody note. The local AI can put the measured numbers into words, but the numbers come from tested analysis, never from a guess.
  • Practice over time: takes are kept and compared ("in tune 82% of the time, up from 70% last week"), and practice exercises are suggested from what went wrong: a phrase looped at a slower tempo, a sustained note, a breathing exercise.
  • Privacy: all of it runs on the user's machine; nothing of the recording leaves it.
  • How to build it: pitch tracking of the take (YIN or the pitch detection behind Convert to MIDI), note alignment to the melody, breath and level detection, in src/generate or src/analysis, tested on made-up takes: sines with known pitch errors, slides, gaps and vibrato.
  • Ties in with: A singing techniques guide (the advice links to its pages), Sing along, the lyrics guide's Singing along, Detect lyrics from audio, Generate MIDI from the selected audio track, takes and comping, and Guide paths by goal (the Sing path).

Humanize MIDI with AI

Priority 1

Make a MIDI clip sound played instead of programmed: small shifts in timing, velocity and length that follow how a player of that instrument plays. Generated parts land on a 1/16 grid with few velocities, and so do clips drawn by hand; Humanize... on a clip (or the selected notes) would fix that, with an amount slider and a preview before applying.

  • Piano: shape the velocity along each phrase (rising to its peak, falling at its end), bring the melody out over the accompaniment, play chords slightly spread (the lowest note a few ms first), lean on the downbeats, rush or hold back a little around phrase ends, and give legato notes a small overlap. Pedal (CC64) per chord change would help most, once clips support controllers.
  • Guitar strumming: turn block chords into strums: stagger the notes 5-30 ms in string order (down strokes low to high, up strokes high to low, and softer), voice the chord as a guitar shape (six strings, standard tuning), alternate down and up strokes with the rhythm, add muted ghost strums, and let strings ring over the next stroke. Picked arpeggios: let notes ring into each other.
  • Drums: the classic case: ghost notes and accents on the snare, hi-hat velocities that breathe with the beat, a pushed or laid-back feel per drum, and a bit of swing. Magenta's GrooVAE (Apache 2.0, trained on the Groove MIDI Dataset of real drummers, CC BY 4.0) does exactly this and is small; it's the "Groove" tool in Magenta Studio, and could run through ONNX Runtime like Basic Pitch.
  • Bass, strings, brass, vocals-like leads: articulation (shorter and longer notes), slides and small pitch bends where clips get pitch bend, and breathing room at phrase ends.
  • How (from simple to AI):
    • Rules first, which already go a long way and run instantly: random-but-correlated timing (drifting, not jittery), velocity curves per beat position and phrase, chord spread and strum order. Research on expressive performance (the KTH rule system) gives proven starting rules.
    • Learned models per instrument: GrooVAE for drums; expressive piano models trained on real performances (VirtuosoNet and later ones, many trained on the MAESTRO recordings, which are non-commercial); check licences before shipping any.
    • The local LLM: send the notes with the instrument and style and ask for per-note offsets and velocities through a JSON schema like the generators use. Cheap to try, though less precise than a trained model.
  • Style and feel: options for feel (tight, relaxed, laid back, pushed), swing amount, and genre (a jazz pianist, a pop ballad, a funk strummer), so the same chords can be strummed like a campfire guitar or played like a ballad piano.
  • Could also run straight after generating (a Humanize checkbox in the Generate panel), and on recorded parts in reverse: Quantize that keeps the feel.

Generate samples and loops from a text prompt

Type "dusty lo-fi drum loop, 80 BPM" or "warm evolving pad in A minor" and get a short audio clip to use in your song. The model is Stable Audio Open, run on the user's own machine as an optional download. It makes sounds, not songs, so it suits drum hits, loops, ambiences, risers and one-shot instrument samples. For whole parts that follow the chord track, see WAV generation (vocal and instrumental).

  • What it does: a Generate sample... window (AI menu, and the right-click menu of an empty audio track). Inputs are a prompt, a duration, a negative prompt (what to avoid, such as "vocals"), a seed (the same seed and prompt give the same clip), steps, guidance scale (how closely it follows the prompt) and, optionally, starting audio from a clip (audio-to-audio, with an init noise level for how far the result may drift from it). The result is stereo 44.1 kHz, up to about 47 s with the full model and about 11 s with Stable Audio Open Small. Each result is audible before it is kept, and can go onto an audio track as a clip or into a sampler as a sample.

Pitch, key and tempo are only nudged by the prompt, not guaranteed, so the result is not tuned: check it with the tuner and the tempo tools, then stretch or pitch-shift it. It is not a playable instrument (no note input).
  • Prompt from the song: DawIt can fill part of the prompt from the song: the tempo ("90 BPM"), the key ("A minor") and the mood of the section the clip is for. The user sees the prompt and can change it before generating.

Tooltips say what steps and guidance do in plain words.

Drum tracks: a drum view and drum notation

A drum part in the piano roll is a column of anonymous keys; drummers and beginners think in kick, snare and hi-hat. Two ways to show a drum track that fit it better, for MIDI drum tracks (the General MIDI drum map, or the drum kit's own) and, later, for drums found in audio:

  • A drum view (drum editor): one named lane per drum that the part uses (kick, snare, closed and open hi-hat, toms, crash, ride), in the order of a kit (cymbals on top, kick at the bottom, as on a drum staff), instead of 128 piano keys. Hits drawn as diamonds or blocks with their velocity as size or colour, a grid of steps (ties in with Step editor), the beats and bars numbered, clicking a lane's name plays that drum, and an empty lane can be added from a menu of the kit's drums. Ghost notes (quiet snare hits), flams and rolls drawn so you can see them.
  • Drum notation: the same part as a drummer reads it: a five-line percussion staff with the kick in the bottom space, the snare in the third space, hi-hat and cymbals as x note heads above the staff, the hi-hat foot below; stems up for the hands and down for the feet, beams per beat, rests, accents for loud hits and brackets for ghost notes; open hi-hat with a circle. Read-only first (a printable page for a drummer, or to learn to read drum charts), editable later.
  • A drum kit to see: a picture of a typical rock kit as seen from the drummer's seat (or from above): kick drum on the floor, snare between the knees, hi-hat on the left (for a right-handed player) with its foot pedal, rack toms over the kick, the floor tom on the right, crash and ride cymbals above. Each drum in the picture lights up when it plays, in time with the track, and clicking one plays it and shows its lane and its MIDI note. The drum view's lanes and the staff positions use the same colours, so the picture, the lanes and the notation connect.
  • How it is played: which limb plays what (right foot the kick, left foot the hi-hat pedal, right hand the hi-hat or ride, left hand the snare, both hands crossing for fills), the sticking (right-left alternating, paradiddles), the sounds each drum can make (snare: a centre hit, a rimshot, a cross-stick, a ghost note; hi-hat: closed, open, half-open, foot; cymbals: bow, bell, edge, choked), why a real drummer can't play two drums with one hand at once (a check that warns when a programmed part is unplayable), and how the parts of a groove relate: the kick and snare make the backbeat, the hi-hat or ride keeps time, toms and crashes mark fills and new sections. A tracker view (like Scream Tracker)

Another way to see and edit a MIDI clip, as in the trackers of the 90s (Scream Tracker, FastTracker, Impulse Tracker) and their modern successors (Renoise, OpenMPT, the Polyend Tracker, Furnace): time runs down the screen, one row per step (usually a 16th note), and each track is a column of text, edited entirely from the computer keyboard.

  • What a row shows: per column the note (C-4, --- for nothing, === for note off), the instrument, the volume (velocity) and an effect command (a slide, a vibrato, a retrigger, an arpeggio, a delay of a few ticks). Rows are numbered in hex or decimal, every 4th and 16th row highlighted (the beats and bars).
  • Editing: a cursor moves over the cells; the bottom two rows of letters play notes like a piano (Z = C, S = C#, X = D... as in Play notes with the computer keyboard), the octave set with a key; a step size moves the cursor down after each note; Delete clears, Insert and Backspace push rows down or pull them up; copy and paste blocks; transpose a selection. While playing, the rows scroll past a fixed line.
  • Patterns and the order list: classic trackers build a song from numbered patterns (64 rows each) played in an order list; in DawIt that would map to clips on the timeline and the song sections, rather than a second way to arrange the song.
  • Why have it: very fast for drums, basslines, arpeggios and chiptune, everything is exact and visible (no hidden overlaps, every velocity a number), and many electronic musicians learned on one. It also explains MIDI well: a note is a row with a pitch, a velocity and an end.
  • Why maybe not: it overlaps the piano roll and the Step editor idea, it's a niche way of working that most beginners won't look for, and the effect column only makes sense where DawIt's instruments can do slides, vibrato or retriggers (the built-in synth would need them as per-note commands, or they become pitch bend and modulation). Start small: a read-and-edit tracker view of the selected MIDI clip (note, velocity, length as a note-off) next to the piano roll, sharing its selection, and a What is a tracker? page; leave patterns, order lists and effect commands for later.

Analyze the mix and advise on changes

The sound side of Analyze song: render the song (or the loop range) offline, measure each track and the master, and give concrete mixing advice, each point with why, where in the song, and a fix.

  • Clashing frequencies: find tracks that fight in the same range at the same time (kick and bass at 50-100 Hz, guitars and vocals at 1-4 kHz, a build-up of mud at 200-500 Hz across many tracks), from per-track spectra over time. Advice: cut one of them there with an EQ, move a part an octave, or duck the bass under the kick with sidechain compression.
  • Stereo width: per track and for the master, from the correlation and mid/side balance. Advice: keep the bass and kick in mono, pan or widen guitars, pads and backing vocals, warn when the mix collapses badly in mono or is nearly all in the middle, and suggest opposite pans for parts that double each other.
  • Peaks and dynamics: peak against loudness (crest factor) per track and over time. Advice: compress a vocal or bass that jumps around, tame drum transients that eat headroom, limit the master; and the other way round, warn when something is squashed flat.
  • Levels and balance: loudness per track (LUFS), the vocal or lead buried or on top, clipping on the master, the master's loudness against streaming targets (about -14 LUFS), and too little headroom before mastering.
  • Tonal balance: the master's spectrum against a typical curve for the genre (too dark, too harsh, too much low end), and harsh resonances in single tracks.
  • Showing it: a list of findings sorted by how much they matter, each one clickable to jump to the track and the bars; a frequency-over-time view per track with the clashes marked; a stereo field picture (vectorscope) and a level graph over the song.
  • Fixing it: an Apply button per finding that adds or sets the built-in EQ or compressor on the track (see Built-in effects and synths that teach while you use them), with an A/B switch to hear before and after, and the explanation of why it helps.
  • Measuring is plain signal processing (FFT, LUFS per ITU-R BS.1770, correlation), so it runs without AI; the local LLM can turn the measurements into friendlier advice and answer questions about the mix. Needs offline rendering, the same as Bounce to WAV.

The mastering chain, step by step

Explain the typical steps of mastering, the last stage before a song is released: what each step in the chain does, in which order and why, with typical settings. Then let the user build that chain on the master and hear each step. Today the master has only a volume fader and a meter, without a plugin chain; A master track in the arrangement and the mixer adds one.

  • The typical chain, in order, each with what it's for and what to listen for:
    1. Prepare: a mix that peaks around -6 dBFS with no limiter on it, bounced at full resolution (24 or 32-bit float), plus one or two reference tracks in the same style.
    2. Clean-up EQ: a low cut for rumble below about 25-30 Hz, and narrow cuts for any resonance that sticks out. Fix problems before shaping the tone.
    3. Glue compression: a gentle bus compressor (1.5-2:1, slow attack of 10-30 ms, auto or medium release, 1-2 dB of gain reduction) so the mix moves together.
    4. Tonal EQ: broad shelves or a tilt to balance low against high, compared with the reference. Moves of a dB or less.
    5. Multiband compression or dynamic EQ (optional): control one range only when it gets loud, such as a booming bass note, harsh 2-5 kHz or sibilance.
    6. Saturation (optional): a little harmonic colour for warmth and loudness without more peak level.
    7. Stereo image: keep the low end mono (below about 100-150 Hz) for clubs and phones, and widen the top carefully. Check that it still sounds right in mono.
    8. Limiter: sets the final loudness and stops the peaks. Ceiling at -1 dBTP (true peak), so the encoding for streaming doesn't clip.
    9. Dither and export: add dither when going to 16 or 24-bit, and bounce the formats needed (WAV for streaming, 44.1 kHz/16-bit for CD).
  • Loudness, explained: LUFS (integrated, short-term, momentary), true peak and loudness range. Typical targets: streaming services normalise to about -14 LUFS (Spotify, YouTube, Tidal), Apple Music to -16; club and EDM masters often go to -9 to -6 LUFS at the cost of dynamics. Explain why louder isn't better once the platform turns it down again.
  • Order matters, and why: clean up before you compress (a compressor reacts to rumble you can't hear), compress before the limiter (the limiter only catches what's left), and keep the limiter last so nothing after it raises the peaks again.
  • In DawIt:
    • A plugin chain on the master, like a track's, shown in the device bar when the master is selected.
    • A Mastering chain starting point that puts DawIt EQ, DawIt Compressor and the new effects (multiband or dynamic EQ, saturation, stereo width, limiter) on the master in the right order, each with a note on why it's there.
    • A guide that walks through the chain one step at a time while the song plays: bypass a step to hear what it adds, at matched loudness so louder doesn't fool the ear.
    • A loudness meter on the master (integrated LUFS, true peak, loudness range) with the target of the chosen platform, and a reference track to compare at the same loudness.
    • Bounce to WAV could add dither and a loudness check before export.

Bug: scrolling while the song plays jumps back to the playhead

While the song plays, scrolling left or right (scroll wheel, trackpad or the scroll bar) is undone straight away: the view jumps back to the playhead, so you can't look at another part of the song without stopping it.

  • Why it happens: ArrangementView::timerCallback() keeps the playhead in view 30 times a second. Whenever the playhead is off screen it moves the view back to it, and it can't tell a playhead that ran off the edge from a view the user scrolled away.
  • The fix, the way most DAWs do it:
    • follow the playhead only while you haven't scrolled: scrolling by hand (lanes.onScroll, the scroll bar, zooming) turns following off;
    • following comes back on when you press Play or click the ruler or a lane to move the playhead, or when the playhead comes back into view by itself;
    • a Follow playhead toggle (View menu, with a shortcut such as F) to turn following off for good, remembered in the settings (view.followPlayhead);
    • optionally, page-by-page following (jump a screen at a time, as now) or a scrolling view with the playhead fixed in the middle, as a choice.
  • Show it: the toggle ticked in the View menu, and a tooltip on the ruler saying that scrolling stops following until the next Play.
  • Test it: following stops after a manual scroll while playing, and comes back on Play and when the playhead is moved.

Automatic builds and tests on macOS, Windows and Linux

A continuous-integration (CI) workflow that builds DawIt and runs its headless tests on macOS, Windows and Linux after every push, so a change that breaks one system is seen the same day. CLAUDE.md promises that Windows and Linux are "kept buildable" through JUCE, but nothing checks it: the app has only been built and run on macOS. The trigger is Sing this, which runs DiffSinger on the ONNX Runtime and has only been tried on a Mac.

  • What runs (on each system):
    • the CMake configure and build of the DawIt app, with warnings as errors, so the code stays free of compiler warnings on MSVC, GCC and Clang too (the flags in CMakeLists.txt are Clang-style and need an MSVC and GCC equivalent);
    • DawItTests, which must end with All checks passed (they are headless and drive the audio by hand through a fake device, so they need no sound card);
    • DawIt --export-guides <folder>, which saves every guide as a PDF and exits with 1 on a failure: a check that the UI code, fonts and the PDF writer work, with the exit code as the result;
    • later: --screenshots on a virtual display (Xvfb on Linux), to see that the windows lay out the same.
  • Which runners:
    • GitHub Actions, free for a public repository: macos-latest (Apple Silicon), windows-latest (x64) and ubuntu-latest (x64). The Windows ARM64 and Linux aarch64 builds that CMakeLists.txt already has branches for can follow on the ARM runners GitHub offers.
    • The workflow lives in .github/workflows/ci.yml, runs on pushes and pull requests, and uses a matrix of the three systems with fail-fast off, so one broken system doesn't hide the others.
    • Linux needs JUCE's development packages first (ALSA, X11, Freetype, fontconfig, curl, GL; the list is in JUCE's Linux notes), installed with apt-get.
    • The workflow calls CMake directly on Windows, because build.sh is a shell script (Git Bash is there on the runner, so it may also work; the question is below).
  • Caching the pinned downloads:
    • JUCE and the other libraries that CMake fetches (FetchContent), including the ONNX Runtime package (1.30.0, about the size of one archive per system), are cached with actions/cache, keyed by the pinned versions and hashes in CMakeLists.txt, so a build doesn't download them again.
    • A compiler cache (ccache or sccache) keeps rebuilds fast; JUCE is large and a cold build is slow on a runner.
    • The models (Demucs, Whisper, a DiffSinger voice) are not downloaded in CI. Tests that need them are skipped or use a tiny stand-in, and a voice (licensed by its maker) is never stored in the repository.
  • A Windows and Linux checklist to try by hand (CI can't hear or click), kept in the repository as a short list for each release:
    • Audio device: the Audio Settings window lists the system's devices (WASAPI and ASIO on Windows, ALSA and JACK on Linux), playback and recording work, a sample-rate change is accepted, and the app copes with no device at all.
    • File dialogs: Open, Save, Import audio, Import MIDI and Export (WAV, MP3, MIDI, PDF) use native dialogs, remember the last folder, and handle paths with spaces, accents and long names; .dawit projects saved on one system load on another (path separators, drive letters).
    • Plugin scanning: VST3 plugins are found in C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3 and ~/.vst3; a plugin that crashes while scanning doesn't take DawIt down; the scan runs out of the process or is skippable.
    • The Voices folder (Sing this): the folder is found in the user's data folder on each system (not the macOS-style ~/Library path), installing a voice from a zip or a folder works, and so do folder names with spaces, accents, Japanese characters and a ~ (as in a voice called "... ~AIdol~"). Paths with a ~ are not the user's home folder, and on Windows a ~1 is a short name: code must never expand it. Paths longer than 260 characters on Windows need the long-path setting.
    • Singing speed: a 30-second melody takes how long on a slow CPU (a laptop with 4 cores) compared with the Mac; the progress bar and Cancel still react; memory stays reasonable. These times could go in the Sing window's text.
    • The ONNX Runtime library: onnxruntime.dll sits next to the exe on Windows, libonnxruntime.so is found by its rpath on Linux; DirectML or CUDA are never used by accident.
    • Looks: fonts and sizes (the text shrinks to fit), high-DPI scaling at 125% and 150% on Windows, the menu bar (inside the window on Windows and Linux, the system bar on macOS), the Cmd shortcuts as Ctrl, and the keys named in tooltips and guides (Cmd+G says Ctrl+G there).
    • Local AI: Ollama over HTTP on localhost works the same, and Demucs and Whisper run (speed, memory).

  • How to build it:
    • .github/workflows/ci.yml with the matrix, the cache steps and three commands (configure, build, run DawItTests, then --export-guides). Keep it close to build.sh --test so a failure can be reproduced on a Mac.
    • Fix what the first runs turn up, which is the point: Clang-only flags, #include case, POSIX-only calls in src/engine and src/analysis (fork, popen, shell scripts), the Windows min/max macros, text encodings (UTF-8 in juce::File paths), and the vendored libraries' own build needs (Eigen and demucs.cpp, whisper.cpp, ONNX Runtime).
    • Tests: a test that finds the Voices folder and builds a voice path with spaces, accents and a ~ in tests/EngineTests.cpp (it runs on all three systems, so CI is what makes it count); and a test that a project's media paths load with both / and \.
    • No new library. CI uses GitHub's runners and actions, with action versions pinned to a commit.
