What is sound? The waveform explained

Explain, in the waveform of an audio clip, what sound and a waveform are: a guide for beginners that starts from the air and ends at the numbers in a WAV file, using the user's own audio as the example.

Plan: plans/sound-guide-envelope-synth-mono.md

Done

Done

  • The wave editor zooms down to single samples and says what is under the mouse (silence, an attack, a fade, a held sound and its pitch).
  • What is sound? opens a guide with the topics below, each with a live picture using the clip in the editor (following the playhead while the song plays): the air moving in front of a speaker, the clip's outline, the tone's peak and RMS against the dB scale, the cycles counted next to the clip's pitch on the range we hear, harmonics as bars next to the clip's spectrum, samples and steps, phase, and phase cancellation (a copy a time later or flipped, with the comb of frequencies it cancels).
  • A tone (sine, triangle, saw or square, any frequency and level, with a copy shifted in phase or time, or flipped) plays what it shows.
  • The envelope (ADSR) is measured and drawn on the note under the playhead, beside the clip's spectrum in The shape is the timbre, with each time and a line of advice.
  • Checking in mono measures how much a stereo clip loses when left and right are added, overall and in five bands, and Hear in mono plays the song that way on the speakers.
  • Playing the DawIt Synth's own waveforms: decided against. The guide keeps its own plain tone, said to be a stand-in, and points to the Subtractive and Additive guides where the synths play.
  • An audio clip view: select an audio clip and the bottom panel (now only for MIDI) shows its waveform, zoomable from the whole clip down to single cycles and samples. A ? opens the guide; hovering over the waveform names what you see (a drum hit's attack, a sustained note's cycles, silence).
  • The guide, step by step:
    • Sound is moving air: a speaker or a string pushes air molecules together (compression) and pulls them apart (rarefaction); the pressure wave travels to your ear at about 343 m/s. An animation of molecules next to the waveform: the line going up is air being squeezed, down is air thinning out.
    • A waveform is pressure over time: the microphone turns pressure into voltage, and the waveform plots it, left to right in time.
    • Amplitude is loudness: a bigger swing is louder; decibels, why +6 dB doubles the amplitude, peak versus average (RMS) level, and clipping at 0 dBFS (linking to the bounce's peak warning and Gain staging).
    • Frequency is pitch: cycles per second (Hz); A above middle C is 440 Hz, an octave up doubles it; the hearing range 20 Hz to 20 kHz; zoom in on a held note and count its cycles.
    • The shape is the timbre: a sine, a saw and a square at the same pitch sound different because of their harmonics (play each with the DawIt Synth, and see it); a piano and a guitar playing the same note differ in shape and envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release).
    • From air to numbers: sampling (44.1 kHz takes 44,100 measurements a second, why that covers our hearing), bit depth (16 or 24-bit, the steps of each measurement and the noise floor), mono and stereo: the choices in Bounce to WAV explained.
    • Phase: two waves adding up or cancelling, why a kick and bass can thin each other out, and why stereo tracks can vanish in mono.
  • Play with it: sliders to make a tone (frequency, amplitude, waveform shape) and hear and see it change; a spectrum view next to the waveform, showing the harmonics.
  • Ties in with: Built-in effects and synths that teach (the synth's waveforms), Analyze the mix and Gain staging (levels, clipping, phase), stems and audio to MIDI (what the models listen to).

Hearing, monitoring and the room

Why a mix sounds different on headphones, in the car and on a phone comes down to how ears, speakers and rooms work. A set of short guides, linked from What is sound?, would explain what the ear hears, how to read the spectrum, and how to listen in a way that can be trusted.

Done

  • What is sound? already explains air pressure, the waveform, amplitude and dB, frequency, timbre, sampling, and phase and cancellation.
  • What it covers:
    • Hearing: the range of hearing (20 Hz to 20 kHz, shrinking with age), equal-loudness curves (Fletcher-Munson, ISO 226: why quiet mixes sound thin and loud ones better), masking, the missing fundamental (bass on phone speakers).
    • Pitch and frequency: notes to Hz and back, cents, octaves as doublings, the harmonic series as pitch (links to additive synthesis), tuning standards (A440, the 432 Hz myths).
    • The frequency spectrum by ear: sub (20 to 60 Hz), bass, low mids (mud), mids (honk), upper mids (presence, harshness), highs (air), with an example per instrument and ear-training games.
    • Spectrum analysers and spectrograms: how to read them, FFT size against time resolution, why a spectrum isn't what you hear.
    • Acoustics of a room: reflections, standing waves and room modes, bass traps and absorption, why a mix sounds different in the car (links to reverb).
    • Monitoring: speakers against headphones, near-field placement (the triangle), flat response, reference tracks, listening level (about 79 to 85 dB SPL), mono checks.
    • Hearing health: safe listening levels and times, ear fatigue, breaks.
  • Questions it must answer: Why does my song sound good in headphones but bad in the car? Why can't I hear my bass on a phone? Is 432 Hz better? Should I mix on headphones? They become the guide's search words in the Theory Guides window.
  • How to build it: a guide in the usual format (see CLAUDE.md, Theory blocks): content in src/generate/ with topics, a live picture and a Your song topic, an entry in gen::guides, and tests that check its facts.
  • Ties in with: What is sound? The waveform explained, Digital audio in practice: buffers, dither and file formats, Sound placement: width, depth and height, What is mixing? The steps, and common problems and their fixes, A guide icon for every theory popup, and a tempo guide by the BPM.

A tuner for guitar, bass and other instruments

A tuner window that listens to an instrument on an audio input and shows which note it plays and how many cents sharp or flat it is, with presets for guitar, bass, ukulele and other tunings.
